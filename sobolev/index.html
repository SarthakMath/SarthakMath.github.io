Why Sobolev spaces?

Finite element theory is written in spaces called H¹ and H², not in the spaces of smooth functions from calculus. Five experiments show why the smooth spaces are not enough.

The finite element method replaces the equation −u″ = f by its weak form, which involves only the energy ∫(u′)² dx. So the natural question is: which functions have finite energy? The answer is a Sobolev space, called H¹. Each experiment below breaks something that calculus takes for granted, and shows that the energy still makes sense. If the weak form is new to you, read From PDE to matrix first.

1Jumps and corners

A finite element solution is a chain of straight segments. It has corners at the nodes, so it has no second derivative there, and even its first derivative is undefined at each node. Why is that allowed? And if corners are allowed, why not go further and let the pieces be disconnected?

The energy answers both questions. Take a function with a jump, and spread the jump over a small width ε. Then do the same with a corner.

To climb a height of 1 across a width ε the slope must be 1/ε. Squared and integrated over the width, that gives an energy of 1/ε, which grows without bound as the jump sharpens. A corner is different: the slope stays between −2 and 2 however sharp the corner is, and the energy stays below 4.

So the energy draws the line exactly where finite elements need it. Corners cost a finite amount. Jumps cost infinitely much. Functions with finite energy may have corners but not jumps, and that is the reason the hat functions are continuous.

For the experienced reader: the space H¹

H¹(0,1) is the set of u ∈ L²(0,1) that have a weak derivative u′ ∈ L²(0,1), meaning ∫ u φ′ dx = −∫ u′ φ dx for all φ ∈ Cc∞(0,1). In one dimension these are the absolutely continuous functions with square-integrable derivative, and |u(x) − u(y)| ≤ |x − y|1/2 ‖u′‖, so every element of H¹(0,1) is Hölder continuous. The distributional derivative of a jump is a Dirac mass, which is not in L². The general statement, valid in any dimension: a function that is smooth on each element of a mesh belongs to H¹(Ω) if and only if it is continuous across the element interfaces. This is the conformity condition for Lagrange elements.

2Two materials

Join two rods of different metals end to end, hold the outer ends in ice, and heat the whole length evenly. If k(x) is the conductivity, the temperature satisfies

−(k u′)′ = 1  for 0 < x < 1,    u(0) = u(1) = 0,

with k = 1 on the left half and a different constant on the right half. Nothing about this is exotic. Every real structure is made of more than one material.

Heat cannot pile up at the interface, so the flux k u′ must be the same on both sides. Since k jumps, the slope u′ has to jump the other way. The temperature has a corner, and u″ does not exist at the interface. In the sense of calculus this problem has no solution: there is no twice-differentiable function that satisfies the equation.

The weak form does not notice. It asks only for

∫₀¹ k u′ v′ dx = ∫₀¹ v dx    for every test function v,

and a function with a corner has a perfectly good energy, as chapter 1 showed. The finite element solution is computed with no special treatment of the interface, and the continuity of the flux comes out by itself. Try an odd number of elements: the interface then falls inside an element, and the method loses some accuracy but still works.

For the experienced reader: transmission problems

For k ∈ L∞ with k ≥ k₀ > 0 the form a(u, v) = ∫ k u′ v′ dx is bounded and coercive on H¹₀(0,1), and Lax–Milgram gives a unique weak solution. The flux σ = k u′ satisfies σ′ = −f ∈ L², so σ ∈ H¹ is continuous: the transmission condition [k u′] = 0 is a natural condition, contained in the weak form and not imposed. The solution is in H² on each side but not in H²(0,1). When the interface is a mesh node, the Green’s function is piecewise linear on the mesh, the finite element solution is exact at the nodes, and the energy error is O(h). When the interface cuts an element, the best approximation of the kink inside that element limits the energy error to O(h1/2). Meshes should follow material interfaces.

3The missing minimum

The weak solution of −u″ = f is also the function that makes the total energy

J(v) = ½ ∫₀¹ (v′)² dx − ∫₀¹ f v dx

as small as possible. This looks like a way to prove that a solution exists: the energy is bounded below, so take the function with the lowest energy. In the nineteenth century this argument was used freely, until Weierstrass pointed out the gap in it. A set of numbers that is bounded below has a greatest lower bound, but there need not be a function that attains it.

Here is the gap, made visible. Search for the minimum among the smoothest functions there are: sums of sine waves. Add one wave at a time and watch the energy.

Each new wave lowers the energy a little more. The candidates are all infinitely differentiable, and they settle towards a definite shape. But the shape they settle towards is not smooth. With heating on the right half its second derivative jumps. With a point source it has a corner. The smooth functions have a lowest energy level that they approach and never reach, because the function that would attain it is not one of them.

The cure is to enlarge the space until nothing is missing: include every function that a sequence of smooth functions can approach in energy. The result is the Sobolev space H¹₀. In it, the minimum is always attained, and existence of a solution becomes a theorem.

For the experienced reader: completeness

The space Cc∞(0,1) with the energy inner product (u, v) ↦ ∫ u′v′ dx is not complete, and H¹₀(0,1) is by definition its completion. Completeness is what the Riesz representation theorem and the Lax–Milgram lemma require, and it is the only property of H¹₀ they use. For the sine candidates uN, Galerkin orthogonality gives J(uN) − J(u) = ½ |u − uN|²H¹. The gap decays like N−3 for the step heating, where u ∈ H², and only like N−1 for the point source, where u ∈ H3/2−ε. The point source is admissible in one dimension because δ ∈ H−1(0,1); chapter 4 shows that this fails in two dimensions. Hilbert’s rescue of the Dirichlet principle in 1900 is, in modern terms, the direct method of the calculus of variations in a complete space.

4A point load

Engineers like point loads: a force applied at one node, a heat source concentrated at one spot. On a rod this is harmless. On a plate it is not, and Sobolev theory predicts the difference before any computation.

Below, the same experiment is run twice. A unit heat source sits at the centre of a rod of length 2, and at the centre of a disc of radius 1. The boundary is held at zero. Refine the mesh and watch the temperature at the source.

On the rod, every mesh gives the same height, and it is the exact one. On the disc the computed height grows every time the mesh is refined, by the same amount for each halving of h. It never settles. The computation is not wrong: the exact solution is −ln(r)/2π, which is infinite at the source, and its energy is infinite too.

The reason is a fact about finite-energy functions. On a line, they are all continuous, so “the value at a point” is meaningful and a point load is a legitimate load. In the plane, a function can have finite energy and still be unbounded, so its value at a point means nothing, and a point load asks for something the theory cannot supply. A number read off at a point load in two or three dimensions depends on the mesh, and on nothing else.

For the experienced reader: Sobolev embedding

Hs(Ω) ⊂ C(Ω̄) holds for s > d/2, where d is the dimension. For s = 1 this is true only when d = 1. In two dimensions v(x) = ln ln(1/|x|) lies in H¹ of the disc of radius ½ and is unbounded, so point evaluation is not a bounded functional on H¹: δ ∉ H−1(Ω) for d ≥ 2, and Lax–Milgram does not apply. The Green’s function belongs to W1,p only for p < d/(d − 1). For the Galerkin solution, a(uh, uh) = uh(0), so the peak and the energy are the same number, and both behave like (1/2π) ln(1/h). The figure uses radial linear elements on n rings, which is the Galerkin method in the subspace of radial functions. On a triangular mesh the growth rate is the same. Away from the source the approximation still converges, which is why point loads remain usable in practice as long as nothing is read off at the load itself.

5Smoothness sets the speed

Sobolev spaces also answer the practical question: how fast does the error fall when the mesh is refined? For linear elements the textbook answer is “proportional to h in the energy norm”. That answer assumes the solution has two derivatives with finite energy. When it does not, the method slows down, and the theory says by exactly how much.

Take a solution that behaves like xα near the left end, with α between ½ and 1. Its slope is infinite at x = 0, but its energy is finite. Solutions of this kind are not artificial: they appear at every re-entrant corner of a domain and at every crack tip.

With equal elements the error falls like hα − ½. The exponent is the number of derivatives beyond the first that still have finite energy, counted in fractions. Lower α and the line in the lower plot flattens until refinement is almost useless. Nearly all of the error sits in the one element that touches x = 0.

The same theory gives the repair. Crowd the elements towards the singular point, by the right amount, and the full rate returns with the same number of unknowns. This is what adaptive mesh refinement does automatically. Knowing which Sobolev space the solution lives in tells you how fast the method converges and where the mesh must be fine.

For the experienced reader: fractional regularity and graded meshes

The function u = xα(1 − x) belongs to Hs(0,1) exactly for s < α + ½. Céa’s lemma with the interpolation estimate |u − Ihu|H¹ ≤ C hs−1 |u|Hs, 1 ≤ s ≤ 2, gives every rate below α − ½, and the figure shows that the rate α − ½ is attained. In one dimension with constant coefficients the finite element solution coincides with the nodal interpolant, so the errors plotted are exact up to quadrature. The graded mesh has nodes xi = (i/n)μ with μ > 1/(α − ½), which restores O(n−1). In two dimensions the counting shifts by half a derivative: at a corner of interior angle ω > π the solution of the Poisson problem behaves like rπ/ω sin(πθ/ω), which is in H1+s for s < π/ω. On the L-shaped domain π/ω = 2/3, so linear elements on quasi-uniform meshes converge like h2/3 in the energy norm and h4/3 in L², whatever the smoothness of the data.

Where this goes next

The five experiments make one point from five sides. The energy decides which functions are admissible, the admissible functions form a complete space, and the position of the solution in the scale of Sobolev spaces decides how well any mesh can approximate it.
