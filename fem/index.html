From PDE to matrix

How the finite element method turns a differential equation into a linear system a computer can solve, one idea at a time.

Every figure on this page is live. Drag the handles, move the sliders and step through the assembly. Each chapter ends with a box for readers who already know the theory and want the precise statements.

1The problem

A thin metal rod has both ends held in ice. Along its length it is heated, more in some places than in others. Once everything has settled, what is the temperature at each point?

Call the heating rate f(x) and the temperature u(x), with x running from 0 to 1 along the rod. Physics gives one equation and two end conditions:

−u″(x) = f(x)  for 0 < x < 1,    u(0) = u(1) = 0.

The second derivative measures how sharply the temperature profile bends. The equation says the profile bends most where the heating is strongest. This is the simplest elliptic problem, and everything in this page generalises from it.

For these three heating patterns the solution can be written down exactly, which makes them good test cases: we will always know the right answer. For most real problems nobody can write the solution down, and that is why a numerical method is needed. The pattern you pick here is used in every figure below.

For the experienced reader: classical and weak solutions

For continuous f the problem has a unique classical solution u ∈ C², given by the Green’s function: u(x) = ∫₀¹ G(x, s) f(s) ds with G(x, s) = x(1 − s) for x ≤ s and s(1 − x) otherwise. For f ∈ L²(0,1) the equation holds almost everywhere with u ∈ H²(0,1) ∩ H¹₀(0,1) and ‖u″‖ = ‖f‖. For rougher data, such as a point source, only the weak formulation of the next chapter makes sense.

2The weak form

A computer cannot store a function, only a finite list of numbers. So we will replace u by something simple: a chain of straight segments. But a chain of segments has no second derivative at its joints, so the equation −u″ = f cannot even be asked of it.

The way out is to stop demanding the equation at every point, and demand it on average instead, against a whole family of test functions. Multiply both sides by any function v that vanishes at both ends, and integrate along the rod:

−∫₀¹ u″ v dx = ∫₀¹ f v dx.

Integrate the left side by parts. The boundary term u′v disappears, because v(0) = v(1) = 0:

∫₀¹ u′ v′ dx = ∫₀¹ f v dx    for every such v.

One derivative has moved from u onto v. Only first derivatives appear now, and straight segments do have those.

The two shaded regions look nothing alike, yet their areas agree for every test function you try. That is the weak form: infinitely many averaged statements instead of one statement per point. Now choose v = x, which does not vanish at the right end. The balance fails, and it fails by exactly the boundary term we dropped.

For the experienced reader: the functional setting

Take V = H¹₀(0,1), a(u, v) = ∫₀¹ u′v′ dx and ℓ(v) = ∫₀¹ f v dx. The form a is bounded and, by the Poincaré inequality, coercive on V, so the Lax–Milgram lemma gives a unique u ∈ V with a(u, v) = ℓ(v) for all v ∈ V, for any f ∈ H⁻¹(0,1). Because a is symmetric, u is also the unique minimiser of the energy J(v) = ½ a(v, v) − ℓ(v) over V.

3Hat functions

Now make u simple. Cut the rod at a few points, the nodes, and allow only functions that are straight between neighbouring nodes. Such a function is fixed by its heights at the nodes, so it really is a finite list of numbers. The pieces between nodes are the elements.

There is a tidy way to write these functions. For each node i, let φi be the “hat”: equal to 1 at node i, equal to 0 at every other node, and straight in between. Then every chain of segments is a sum of hats,

uh(x) = U1 φ1(x) + U2 φ2(x) + … ,

and the coefficient Ui is simply the height at node i.

Drag a round handle up or down to change one coefficient. Only that hat stretches, and the function changes only in the two elements that touch the node. Drag a diamond on the axis to move a node sideways. The dashed curve is the exact solution, shown for comparison: the aim of the next three chapters is to find the heights that follow it, without knowing it in advance.

For the experienced reader: the discrete space

The hats at the interior nodes span Vh ⊂ H¹₀(0,1), the conforming P1 space. For the nodal interpolant Ihu of u ∈ H², ‖u − Ihu‖ + h ‖(u − Ihu)′‖ ≤ C h² ‖u″‖, with h the longest element. Each hat is supported on two elements only; this local support is what makes the matrix of chapter 5 sparse.

4One element

Put uh = Σ Uj φj into the weak form, and test it with each hat in turn, v = φi:

Σj Uj ∫₀¹ φj′ φi′ dx = ∫₀¹ f φi dx    for each i.

This is a linear system K U = F, with Kij = ∫ φi′ φj′ dx and Fi = ∫ f φi dx. The unknowns are the heights Uj.

An integral over the rod is a sum of integrals over the elements, and on any one element only two hats are non-zero: one falling from 1 to 0, one rising from 0 to 1. So all the work reduces to one small calculation per element. On an element of length h the two slopes are −1/h and +1/h. They are constant, so each integral is just slope × slope × length.

Element matrix. Select an entry.

Element load

Make the element shorter and the entries grow: a hat that climbs over a short distance is steep. Each row of the matrix adds up to zero, because the two hats add up to 1 on the element and a constant has zero slope. The load entries depend on where the element sits, since they sample the heating there.

For the experienced reader: the element matrix

Mapped from a reference element, Ke = (1/h) [[1, −1], [−1, 1]]. It is symmetric positive semidefinite with kernel spanned by (1, 1): before boundary conditions, a(·,·) is only a seminorm. Zero row sums express the partition of unity. In two dimensions the same computation on a triangle gives the cotangent formula, and an off-diagonal entry is non-positive exactly when the two angles opposite the edge sum to at most π. The load uses a three-point Gauss rule, exact for f of degree up to four on each element.

5Assembly

Each element produces a 2 × 2 matrix for its own two nodes. Assembly adds every one of them into the big matrix K, at the rows and columns of those two nodes. Where two elements share a node, their contributions add up.

Element matrix and load

Global matrix K and load F. Rows and columns are numbered by node.

Step through it. Element e joins nodes e − 1 and e, so its block lands on those two rows and columns. The diagonal entry of a shared node receives two contributions, one from the element on each side. Everything away from the diagonal stays empty: two hats that do not overlap never meet inside an integral.

In one dimension the result has three non-zero diagonals. In two and three dimensions the same process leaves a very large matrix that is almost entirely zeros, which is why finite elements scale to millions of unknowns.

For the experienced reader: structure of K, and where it breaks

Assembly is K = Σe Peᵀ Ke Pe with Boolean local-to-global maps Pe; K is the Gram matrix of the hats in the energy inner product. It has positive diagonal, non-positive off-diagonal entries and zero row sums. After the boundary conditions it is an irreducibly diagonally dominant Z-matrix, hence an M-matrix: K⁻¹ ≥ 0 entrywise. That is the discrete maximum principle: f ≥ 0 implies uh ≥ 0.

Add a convection term, −εu″ + bu′ = f, and the Galerkin off-diagonals become −ε/h ± b/2. One of them turns positive as soon as the mesh Péclet number |b|h / (2ε) exceeds 1, the M-matrix property is lost, and the discrete solution oscillates. Restoring the sign structure algebraically, without smearing the solution, is the idea behind algebraic flux correction. The CDR lab shows this in two dimensions.

6Boundary conditions and solving

As assembled, K U = F does not yet have a unique solution: nothing in it says that the ends of the rod are held at zero. But that means the first and last heights are already known, U0 = UN = 0. Remove their rows and columns. What remains is a system for the interior heights only. Solve it and join the dots.

Halving the elements

Two things to notice. At the nodes, the computed heights sit on the exact curve; any difference comes only from integrating f approximately. The error lives between the nodes, where a straight segment cannot follow a curve. And each time the number of elements doubles, that error falls by a factor of about 4.

For the experienced reader: why the nodes are exact, and how fast it converges

Galerkin orthogonality, a(u − uh, vh) = 0 for all vh ∈ Vh, makes uh the best approximation of u in the energy norm (Céa’s lemma, with constant 1 here). In one dimension the Green’s function G(xi, ·) is piecewise linear with its kink at the node xi, so it lies in Vh, and u(xi) − uh(xi) = a(u − uh, G(xi, ·)) = 0. With exact load integrals uh is therefore the nodal interpolant of u. This is special to one dimension and constant coefficients.

In general, ‖(u − uh)′‖ ≤ C h ‖u″‖ and, by the Aubin–Nitsche duality argument, ‖u − uh‖ ≤ C h² ‖u″‖. The condition number of the reduced K grows like h⁻², which is what makes large problems hard to solve iteratively without preconditioning.
